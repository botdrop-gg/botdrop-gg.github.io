Free Discord Bot Hosting 24/7: Your Options for $0

By BotDrop · Updated November 3, 2026 · Free guide, no purchase needed

You can keep a Discord bot online 24/7 without paying for hosting. The catch is that "free" means different things depending on where the bot runs. This guide compares the $0 options side by side: the PC you already have, an old laptop or mini PC, a Raspberry Pi, and the free tiers some cloud services offer. It's honest about what each one is good for and where it falls short.

The short answer

A Discord bot is a small program that stays connected to Discord. It needs a computer that stays on and online. It doesn't need a website, a public address or open ports. That's why a spare computer at home is usually the best free option, and why many "free hosting" offers don't fit a bot as well as they seem to.

Every $0 option at home has one small running cost: electricity. A laptop, mini PC or Raspberry Pi uses far less than a gaming desktop, so if you have a choice, run the bot on the smallest machine you own.

Option 1: The PC you already have

This is the easiest start. There's nothing to sign up for, and you'll know the bot works before you think about anything else. A desktop that stays on most of the day already gives you most of the uptime a small server needs. Leave it on overnight, and it's 24/7.

The trade-off is that your bot shares the computer with you. A restart for a Windows update, a shutdown before a trip or a power cut takes the bot offline until the PC is back. The checklist further down fixes most of that. For the full setup, follow our PC hosting guide.

Option 2: An old laptop or mini PC as a bot box

A laptop you've stopped using makes a good 24/7 bot host. It has a built-in battery that acts like a small backup power supply, and it uses much less power than a desktop. A small office mini PC works the same way.

  • Keep it plugged in and stop it sleeping. On Windows 11, go to Settings → System → Power & battery and set sleep to Never when plugged in.
  • Change what closing the lid does. In Windows, search the Start menu for Change what closing the lid does (it opens the classic Power Options page) and set "When I close the lid" to Do nothing when plugged in. Then you can close it and put it on a shelf.
  • Look after the battery. Many laptops have a charge-limit option in the maker's own settings app, often 80%. Turn it on if yours has one. If an old battery looks swollen, take it out or retire the laptop.
  • Use a cable if you can. Wired internet drops less often than Wi-Fi, so the bot reconnects less.

Option 3: A Raspberry Pi

A Raspberry Pi is a credit-card-sized Linux computer. It's silent and uses very little power, so it's a popular way to keep a bot running around the clock. Bots written in Python or JavaScript (Node.js) usually run fine on Raspberry Pi OS, as long as their libraries install on it.

  • Use the official power supply or a good one of the same rating. A weak charger causes random crashes that look like bot bugs.
  • The microSD card is the weak spot. Keep the bot's saved data small, and copy its settings file and data folder somewhere else from time to time.
  • Run the bot as a service so it starts on boot and restarts after a crash. There's an example in the checklist below.

We haven't tested BotDrop bots on a Raspberry Pi. Our Linux instructions are still labelled beta, so treat a Pi as "should work, try it first".

Option 4: Free cloud tiers (read the limits)

Some cloud services have free plans, and it's tempting to put a bot there. Before you do, know what a bot needs: one program that runs nonstop and holds a connection open to Discord. A lot of free plans are built for websites, and websites work differently.

  • Many free web plans go to sleep. They shut down when no one visits the site and wake up on the next visit. A bot doesn't get site visits, so it just goes offline. Render's documentation, for example, says a free web service spins down after 15 minutes with no inbound traffic. Its free plan doesn't include background workers, the service type an always-on bot normally uses.
  • Files can disappear. On many free plans, anything the bot saves, like strikes, settings or "already posted" lists, is wiped on every restart or redeploy. Then the bot forgets its data or posts the same alerts again.
  • "Keep-alive" tricks are fragile. Some tutorials suggest pinging a free web service every few minutes to keep it awake. That still loses files on restart, it can run into monthly free-hour or usage limits, and it may break the service's terms.
  • Free credits run out. Trial credit is a free start, not free hosting. Know what happens when it ends: does the bot stop, or does your card get charged?
  • "Always free" virtual servers exist, but they're more work. Some large cloud companies offer a small virtual server at no cost. You usually need a card to sign up, you look after a Linux server yourself, and the terms can change. Check the provider's current terms before you rely on one.
  • Be careful with "free bot hosting" sites. Any service that runs your bot needs your bot token, and the token is the bot's password. Only give it to services you trust, and reset it straight away in the Developer Portal if you stop using one.

Free tiers can be fine for testing. For a bot your members rely on, a spare computer at home is usually the more reliable $0 option.

Make any $0 setup survive 24/7

Whichever computer you use, these steps decide whether the bot stays up for a week or for a day:

  1. No sleep. The screen can turn off, but the computer itself must not sleep or hibernate.
  2. Start the bot on boot or login. On Windows, use Task Scheduler → Create Task, with the trigger At log on (or At startup). On the Settings tab, tick If the task fails, restart every 1 minute so a crash doesn't keep the bot down.
  3. Plan for Windows updates. Set Active hours under Settings → Windows Update → Advanced options so restarts happen when your server is quiet. With step 2 in place, the bot comes back on its own afterward.
  4. Come back after a power cut. Many desktops and mini PCs have a BIOS/UEFI setting, often called Restore on AC Power Loss or similar, that turns the computer back on when power returns.
  5. Save state to disk. A bot that remembers what it already posted won't repeat old alerts after a restart.
  6. Check it from your phone. If the bot shows as offline in your member list, the computer is off, asleep or disconnected.

On Linux, including a Raspberry Pi, a small systemd service handles steps 2 and 5 for you. Save this as /etc/systemd/system/mybot.service, changing the paths and user name to yours:

[Unit]
Description=My Discord bot
After=network-online.target
Wants=network-online.target

[Service]
User=youruser
WorkingDirectory=/home/youruser/mybot
ExecStart=/home/youruser/mybot/.venv/bin/python bot.py
Restart=always
RestartSec=10

[Install]
WantedBy=multi-user.target

Then run sudo systemctl enable --now mybot to start it now and on every boot, and journalctl -u mybot -f to watch its log.

When a paid host makes sense (optional)

You never need a paid host for 24/7 uptime. A computer left on does the same job for free. A host is worth it when you'd rather not keep a machine running at home, when your power or internet drops often, or when you want the bot to stay up while you're away for weeks. A small bot costs roughly $5–$8 a month. Our Discord bot hosting guide covers both: free on your PC, or step-by-step on a host.
